2023 to 24Numbers-minded

The hospital's back office

My first proper look inside a hospital wasn't from the ward. It was from the founder's office at Nobel Hospital in Patna, where the questions were about energy bills, ESG reporting and which local campaign actually brought patients through the door. Nobody mentions this side of healthcare when they talk about care. But when energy costs came down by 15%, and we worked out which campaign was worth the money, it became obvious how much a hospital's everyday decisions decide how far it can stretch.

Skill it built: turning operational data into cost decisions.

Where hospitals don't reach

This summer I worked with Mahane Foundation on rural healthcare. Ideas

Before you build a clinic, ask who pays in year three.

A short piece written after this summer's work with Mahane Foundation.

Most conversations about rural healthcare start with a building. A clinic, a few beds, a ribbon-cutting. It makes sense: a building is something you can photograph, fund and point to.

But the building is the easy part. This summer I researched how 20+ NGO healthcare models and CSR-backed programmes run primary care, and the difference between a clinic that lasts and one that quietly goes dark has very little to do with construction. I now think three questions should be answered before the first brick is laid.

Who pays in year three? Grants and CSR cycles are generous at launch and thin later. If the plan only works while the funding is fresh, it isn't a plan. It's a pilot.

Who runs it when the founder moves on? A clinic that depends on one motivated person lasts exactly as long as that person's motivation, or posting. Protocols, trained staff and simple records outlast enthusiasm.

How will we know it's working? Footfall tells you people came. It doesn't tell you whether a blood pressure got controlled, whether a child got the second dose, or whether anyone came back. Answering that needs decent data, and decent data is mostly unglamorous work: asking clearer questions so the answers can actually be used. I got a taste of this redesigning healthcare questionnaires, and it changed how I read any report full of confident numbers.

None of this is glamorous. Nobody inaugurates a follow-up protocol. But if you want a rural clinic to still be open and useful five years from now, this is where the work is, and it's the part of healthcare I want to spend my career on.

Reflection

What building this page taught me.

I started by writing down every adjective I'd use for myself, then crossing out the ones I couldn't back with a number, a date or a place. Most of them went. The three that survived (ground-first, numbers-minded, steady) are the ones on this page, each with its own colour so you can follow it from the first screen to the last.

The hardest part was leaving things out. Market research, music and cricket all matter to me, but a page that says six things says nothing, so each got a supporting role. If you only remember one line, I'd like it to be this: I want to work on the management side of healthcare, and I like to start from the ground.
